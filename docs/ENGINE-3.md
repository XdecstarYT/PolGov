# Engine 3 — World & Geopolitics

Coverage against the 150-feature brief, written to be useful rather than
flattering. Same three categories as `ENGINE-1.md`.

Roughly **112 built, 25 light, 13 not built**.

---

## The organising idea

Every other engine in this game is downstream of a decision the player took.
Engine 3 is not. Most of what happens in it has nothing to do with Verdana,
and the player's job is not to prevent any of it — it is to notice which parts
reach them and how.

That produces the one thing this engine has that the others do not: **actions
whose outcome the player cannot control on the day**. A resolution can be put
and not passed. A crisis can be answered and still escalate. An assessment can
be commissioned and be wrong. Each of those is a mechanic rather than a
frustration, because in each case the outcome was decided years earlier by
things the player *did* control.

## Diplomacy (1–20)

Thirty-six real modern states, plus one invented country to learn the
machinery in. Each carries what is true about it regardless of who is asking
— output, population, debt, defence burden, the industries it sells and buys,
who it borders, which institutions it belongs to — and every figure is an
approximation of a real one.

The *relational* half is computed rather than stored, because it is only ever
true from somewhere. Who counts as a neighbour, whose economy is larger than
yours, who buys what you sell, who starts out warm: all of it is worked out
from the capital the player is sitting in, which is the property that makes
the same table playable from any of them. A border reads the same from both
sides; an economy does not, and should not.

Nothing in the table is a named living politician. Offices, not people —
partly because putting invented words in a real person's mouth is a different
thing from modelling a country, and partly because a run lasts sixteen years
and incumbents do not.

Relations drift toward what ideology, geography, institutions and trade imply;
an embassy does not improve them, it slows their decay.

Reputation is earned by keeping agreements and lost by breaking them, and it
is read by *every* country rather than the one that was let down — which is
why withdrawing from a treaty is expensive in a way the other signatory never
has to enforce.

Everything is scaled by how much the other country's opinion is worth. A
protest to Washington is a diplomatic event; a protest to Wellington is a
letter. That asymmetry is not a puzzle with a solution; it is the position a
middling country is in.

## International organisations (21–40)

Fourteen real bodies — the General Assembly, the Security Council, the ICC,
the WTO, the IMF and the World Bank, NATO, the EU, the G7 and G20, BRICS,
OPEC, the African Union and ASEAN. Nobody writes the membership lists: each
room is filled from the country table, so adding a state to the world puts it
in the right rooms and the two can never disagree.

Dues are assessed as a share of output rather than a flat sum, which is how
every one of these bodies actually bills and why the same membership is a
rounding error for one member and a line the finance ministry argues about for
another. They are payable every week whether or not the room was used, return
less than they cost in any single week and more over a term, and constrain
what the government may do. Admission is by consent, so the test is the member
who likes you least rather than the average of the room.

**You may put a resolution; you may not pass one.** Every member votes its own
interests and each vote comes back with its reason, so a loss tells the player
which relationships they did not build. An assembly wants two thirds of those
voting; a room that declines to have an opinion defeats a resolution rather
than waving it through. Five permanent members hold a veto on the Council and
most countries are not one of them.

## Trade (41–60)

**Gravity rather than goodwill.** How much two countries trade is mostly
decided by how big they are and how close — a neighbour trades far more than
its size implies, and no policy changes that. Relations move a quarter either
way at the extremes, which is a great deal of money and nothing like a
doubling. Policy works on the margin, and the margin is where the politics is.

A tariff shelters the industry it protects and is paid by everybody who buys
anything. The partner answers six weeks later, by which time the domestic
benefit has been announced and the cost has not arrived — that lag is the
entire political economy of protection and it is modelled explicitly. An
assertive government answers with a larger tariff; an institutional one files
a complaint, which is slower and worse for a government that cares what the
world thinks.

Dependence is leverage in whichever direction it runs, and the exposure list
names the partners the country could not replace before the crisis rather than
after it.

## The forces (61–80)

Strength is what gets announced. **Readiness** is what decides anything, and
it is the first thing cut because the saving is immediate and nothing visible
happens. **Equipment** falls every week whatever anybody does. The three
multiply rather than add, so a large unready force with old kit is a budget
line and money cannot buy a way out of neglect.

Procurement is late and over budget because it is, always, everywhere. A
programme is a third late on the day it is announced and runs on a clock that
does not reset at an election, so a successor collects it. The interesting
question is never which to buy; it is whether to begin something that will be
finished by somebody else, in a region whose jobs make cancelling it a
political decision rather than a financial one.

Four doctrines, none better than another. Deployment costs what it uses:
forces committed abroad are not available at home, wear out faster than money
repairs them, and produce veterans, who are a constituency rather than a
statistic.

## Conflict (81–100)

An escalation ladder — incident, standoff, crisis, war — with asymmetric
politics on every rung. Going up is cheap, popular and fast. Coming down is
expensive, unpopular and slow. The asymmetry is the mechanic rather than a
balance problem.

Approval rises when a crisis begins and falls further than it rose if it does
not end. Measured over a hundred and twenty weeks: up four points in the first
quarter, negative by the ninth month, and twenty-six points down by the time a
losing war ends.

What decides a war is the force ratio, the alliances and the resolve, all set
by budgets passed years before. **The player never fights.** Deterrence shows
up as a crisis that goes nowhere, which is an absence, which nobody has ever
been thanked for.

A mutual defence treaty is the one agreement that can commit the country to a
war it did not choose. When a war starts between two other countries and one
of them is a country Verdana promised to defend, the quarrel becomes this
country's — and it does not start at the bottom of the ladder, because the
choice to honour the promise or not was made in public the moment the war
began.

## Intelligence (101–120)

The only system in the game whose output the player cannot trust, which is the
most accurate thing about it. Assessments are the truth plus noise scaled by
how well the country can see the question, labelled with a confidence that is
itself an estimate.

Capability can be counted and is wrong two per cent of the time. Intentions
cannot be collected against at all, only inferred, and are wrong twenty-eight
per cent of the time — still seven per cent with perfect collection. Analysts
understate the spread most on the questions where an answer is most wanted, so
a high-confidence intentions assessment arrives about one time in thirty and
is badly wrong about a quarter of those.

Operations are deniable until they are not, with the exposure risk fixed at
authorisation rather than at landing. Oversight is an honest trade in both
directions. Surveillance is the only lever here with a constituency on both
sides, and it genuinely reduces what goes wrong at home.

## Global events and the world simulation (121–150)

Countries have relationships with **each other**, not only with Verdana. They
drift, wobble for reasons no briefing captures, collapse within a month once
shooting starts, and occasionally produce a war nobody here voted on. Power
and posture are live, so the trade gravity, the balance of force in a crisis
and the arithmetic of every international vote look different in term four
than they did in term one.

Global events arrive through channels that already exist — the IS curve's
demand term, the Phillips curve's supply term, the trade book's multiplier,
the migration figure, the health sector. Each carries its transmission line:
how a thing that happened somewhere else reaches here. Most offer no response
at all, deliberately.

Measured over sixteen years with nothing the player did: twenty-four of
forty-one relationships moved more than eight points, the furthest by
forty-eight, and a war happened between two countries that never mentioned
this one.

## Light

- Blocs are a property of nations rather than an institution that can be
  joined, led or split.
- Espionage against Verdana is a single penetration figure rather than named
  hostile operations with their own lifecycle.
- Foreign aid exists as a resolution rather than as a standing programme.
- Nuclear deterrence is a state flag with a cost; the decision to build one is
  not yet a multi-term programme with domestic politics attached.
- Peacekeeping deployments are priced but do not model the conflict they are
  deployed into.

## Not built

- A map. Positions are relational — bloc, neighbour, power — rather than
  spatial.
- Colonies, dependencies or contested territory.
- International courts trying individuals.
- Refugee populations tracked as people rather than as a migration rate.
- Arms exports as an industry with a foreign policy attached.
- Climate as a shared global state with negotiated targets, rather than as
  events and a domestic sector.
